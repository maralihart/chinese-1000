Beginner guide · Mandarin · simplified characters

A thousand characters carry most of what you read.

Learn how Chinese characters are built, which ones to meet first, and the handful of sentence patterns that turn vocabulary into conversation. Every new character and term on this page is written with pinyin.

01

Mental model

Characters stack, they are not drawn

Beginners usually treat a character as a picture to memorise. Almost none of them are. A character is assembled from a small set of reusable parts, and most of those parts do one of two jobs: hint at the meaning, or hint at the sound. Learn the ladder once and every later character costs less.

1

Strokes are written in a fixed order

Every character is drawn top to bottom, left to right, horizontal before vertical. Consistent order is what makes handwriting recognition, dictionaries, and your own muscle memory work.

2

Components repeat

A few hundred components (部首 bùshǒu, radicals, and phonetic parts) build thousands of characters. 女 nǚ alone appears in 妈, 好, 她, 姐.

3

Meaning + sound is the default

When you meet a new character, ask which half hints at the meaning and which half hints at the sound. The sound hint is a family resemblance, not a rule.

4

Characters are not words

字 zì is a character; 词 cí is a word. Most modern words are two characters, so 1000 characters unlock far more than 1000 words.

stroke → component → character (字 zì) → word (词 cí) → sentence pattern

Caveat · how firm is this number? Estimates that 80–90% of characters are semantic-phonetic compounds come from sinological analysis rather than one modern study, and the share depends on which character set is counted (Xu Shen's 说文解字 corpus is about 82%). Treat the structure as dominant, not universal: 日 rì (sun) and 上 shàng (above) have no sound part at all.

Break down any new character

  1. Say the pinyin aloud, with the tone.
  2. Split it into left/right or top/bottom parts.
  3. Name the part you already know.
  4. Decide which part is meaning and which is sound.
  5. Write it once in stroke order.
  6. Use it in one sentence you would actually say.
02

Essentials

Six things to settle before character one

Sound

Pinyin and the four tones

Hanyu Pinyin spells Mandarin syllables in Latin letters. Four tones plus a neutral tone change meaning outright: 妈 mā mother, 麻 má hemp, 马 mǎ horse, 骂 mà to scold, 吗 ma question particle.

Tone is part of the word. Learn every character with its tone attached, never as bare letters.

Sound

Two tone changes you will hear immediately

Two third tones in a row: the first is said as a second tone — 你好 is written nǐ hǎo and said ní hǎo.

不 bù becomes bú before a fourth tone: 不是 bú shì. Textbooks usually print the original tone, which is why what you hear can differ from the page.

Script

Simplified or traditional

Mainland China and Singapore use simplified characters, and Chinese-medium schools in Malaysia teach them. Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau use traditional characters. All HSK exam materials are simplified.

This guide is simplified throughout. Pick one script now; switching later is much easier than learning both at once.

Counting

Characters versus words

Chinese text has no spaces. 字 zì is a written character; 词 cí is a word, usually two characters. 电 diàn electric plus 话 huà speech gives 电话 diànhuà, telephone.

A "1000 character" goal and a "1000 word" goal are different targets. This site teaches characters through the words they appear in.

Coverage

What 1000 characters really buys

In a large corpus of written Chinese, the 1000 most frequent characters account for roughly 89% of the characters on the page, and the top 2000 about 97%.

That is coverage of characters, not of meaning: an unknown compound made of two known characters still stops you. Expect fluent decoding, not full comprehension.

Standards

Which official list to aim at

There is no official "top 1000 characters" document. The nearest anchors are the HSK 3.0 grading standard (GF 0025-2021), where Levels 1–4 cumulate to 1,200 characters, and the 2013 Table of General Standard Chinese Characters, whose primary table holds 3,500.

The vocabulary list below is a teaching subset chosen for this site, not an exam list.

Caveat · exam standards are in transition HSK 2.0 (six levels, 150 to 5,000 words) and HSK 3.0 (three stages, nine levels) are both operative as of August 2026. HSK 3.0 took effect on 1 July 2021 and a further global pilot examination is scheduled for 20 September 2026, but no mandatory switchover date has been published. The two systems are not interchangeable, so check the current syllabus at the exam board before you buy books. HSK 2.0 was defined by word lists, so any per-level character count you see for it is an unofficial estimate.

03

Learning path

Six stages to the first thousand

Each stage has one job, one checkable result, and a rough size. Do not move on because time passed; move on when the check passes.

  1. Stage 1Week 1 · 0 characters

    Fix the sound system. Learn pinyin initials, finals, and the four tones before any character.

    Check: you can hear the difference between 买 mǎi (buy) and 卖 mài (sell) four times out of five, and read any pinyin syllable aloud with its tone.

  2. Stage 2Weeks 2–4 · ~100 characters

    Learn strokes and the highest-frequency characters. Write each one by hand at least once in correct stroke order.

    Check: you can write 我, 你, 好, 是, 不, 有, 人, 大, 小, 中 from memory and read them in any order.

  3. Stage 3Weeks 5–10 · ~300 characters

    Add HSK 1 words and the first six sentence patterns. Every new character enters through a word, and every word enters through a sentence.

    Check: you can introduce yourself, ask someone's name, say where you live, and ask a yes/no question without notes.

  4. Stage 4Months 3–5 · ~600 characters

    Add HSK 2 words, time expressions, and aspect. Start reviewing on a spaced schedule instead of re-reading lists.

    Check: you can describe yesterday, today, and tomorrow in three sentences, using 了 le and a time word correctly placed.

  5. Stage 5Months 6–9 · ~900 characters

    Add HSK 3 words, connectors, and comparison. Read graded texts daily; look up only what blocks meaning.

    Check: you can give a reason with 因为…所以… yīnwèi…suǒyǐ… and compare two things with 比 bǐ.

  6. Stage 6Months 10–12 · ~1000+ characters

    Consolidate by reading and speaking, not by adding lists. Mine new characters from texts you actually want to read.

    Check: you can read a short graded story and explain it aloud, and you meet fewer than five unknown characters per page.

Caveat · timings are a plan, not evidence The stage sizes follow the HSK level structure, but the week and month figures are a planning estimate for roughly one focused hour a day. No study quoted here measures how long a specific learner needs. Spaced review itself is strongly supported by memory research (Ebbinghaus 1885; Cepeda et al. 2006); claims that any particular app is optimal for characters are not.

05

Sentence patterns

Twenty structures that unlock conversation

Chinese has no verb conjugation, no gender, and no plural agreement. What it has instead is word order and a small set of function words. Learn these twenty frames and you can recombine your vocabulary into thousands of real sentences. Filter by what you want to do.

Filter by purpose

1. Subject + verb + object

S + V + O

我喝茶。
Wǒ hē chá.
I drink tea.

The backbone of the language. Nothing changes for person or tense, so the same three words work for I, you, they, yesterday, and tomorrow.

2. A 是 B — identity

A + 是 shì + B

他是老师。
Tā shì lǎoshī.
He is a teacher.

是 joins two nouns. Do not use it before an adjective: 我是忙 is wrong.

3. Subject + 很 + adjective

S + 很 hěn + adj

今天很热。
Jīntiān hěn rè.
Today is hot.

Adjectives act as verbs. 很 is the default link and is often barely stressed, so it does not really mean "very" here.

4. 有 — have and there is

S + 有 yǒu + O · negate with 没有

我有两个孩子。
Wǒ yǒu liǎng ge háizi.
I have two children.

有 is the one verb that is never negated with 不.

5. 在 — location

S + 在 zài + place

她在学校。
Tā zài xuéxiào.
She is at school.

Used alone it means "to be at". Placed before another verb it means "doing something at": 我在家吃饭。

6. 吗 — yes/no question

statement + 吗 ma ?

你是学生吗?
Nǐ shì xuésheng ma?
Are you a student?

Word order never changes for questions. Add 吗 and keep everything else.

7. A-not-A question

V + 不 + V

你去不去?
Nǐ qù bu qù?
Are you going or not?

A neutral alternative to 吗, very common in speech. With 有 it becomes 有没有.

8. Question words stay in place

S + V + 什么 / 谁 / 哪儿

你吃什么?
Nǐ chī shénme?
What are you eating?

The question word sits exactly where the answer will sit. No fronting, no 吗.

9. 的 — possession and description

modifier + 的 de + noun

这是我的书。
Zhè shì wǒ de shū.
This is my book.

Anything that describes a noun goes in front of it. Close relationships often drop 的: 我妈妈.

10. Time before the verb

S + time + V + O

我明天去北京。
Wǒ míngtiān qù Běijīng.
I am going to Beijing tomorrow.

The time word can also open the sentence. It can never go at the end.

11. 了 — completion and change

V + 了 le · sentence + 了

我吃了饭。
Wǒ chī le fàn.
I have eaten.

了 is not a past tense. Learn two uses only for now: a completed action, and a new situation (下雨了 — it has started raining).

12. 过 — have ever done

V + 过 guo

我去过中国。
Wǒ qù guo Zhōngguó.
I have been to China.

Experience at some unspecified point in your life. Negate with 没: 我没去过.

13. 正在 — happening right now

正在 zhèngzài + V

他正在打电话。
Tā zhèngzài dǎ diànhuà.
He is on the phone right now.

在 alone does the same job in casual speech.

14. Modal verbs: 想 / 要 / 会 / 能 / 可以

S + modal + V + O

我想喝咖啡。
Wǒ xiǎng hē kāfēi.
I would like to drink coffee.

想 would like, 要 want or will, 会 know how to, 能 physically able, 可以 permitted.

15. 不 versus 没

不 bù + V · 没 méi + V

我不吃肉。我没吃饭。
Wǒ bù chī ròu. Wǒ méi chī fàn.
I do not eat meat. I have not eaten.

不 for habits, preferences, and the future. 没 for things that did not happen.

16. 也 and 都 sit before the verb

S + 也 / 都 + V

我们都是学生。
Wǒmen dōu shì xuésheng.
We are all students.

Never place these at the end of a sentence, and 都 always follows the group it refers to.

17. Number + measure word + noun

number + MW + noun

三个人，两杯茶
sān ge rén, liǎng bēi chá
three people, two cups of tea

You can never count a noun directly. 个 works for most things while you learn the specific ones.

18. 比 — comparison

A + 比 bǐ + B + adj

今天比昨天热。
Jīntiān bǐ zuótiān rè.
Today is hotter than yesterday.

Drop 很 in comparisons. There is no separate comparative form of the adjective.

19. Paired connectors

因为…所以… · 虽然…但是… · 如果…就…

虽然很难，但是很有意思。
Suīrán hěn nán, dànshì hěn yǒu yìsi.
Although it is hard, it is interesting.

Unlike English, Chinese normally keeps both halves of the pair.

20. 请 and 吧 — softening

请 qǐng + V · sentence + 吧 ba

请坐吧。
Qǐng zuò ba.
Please, do have a seat.

请 makes a request polite; 吧 turns an order into a suggestion. Reduplicating a verb softens it too: 看看 kànkan, have a quick look.

Caveat · level labelsThe order of these patterns follows common teaching sequences, including the Chinese Grammar Wiki's A1/A2 grading, which is a publisher's editorial judgement rather than an official syllabus. Individual textbooks and the HSK bands introduce these structures in slightly different orders.

Practical examples

Put it together: three real exchanges

A. Meeting someone

A 你好! 你叫什么名字?
Nǐ hǎo! Nǐ jiào shénme míngzi?
Hello! What is your name?
B 我叫小李。你呢?
Wǒ jiào Xiǎo Lǐ. Nǐ ne?
I am Xiao Li. And you?
A 我叫马丁。认识你很高兴。
Wǒ jiào Mǎdīng. Rènshi nǐ hěn gāoxìng.
I am Martin. Pleased to meet you.

Patterns used: question words in place (8), 的-free naming with 叫, 很 + adjective (3), 呢 for "and you?".

B. Ordering in a shop

A 你好，我要一杯茶。
Nǐ hǎo, wǒ yào yì bēi chá.
Hello, I would like a cup of tea.
B 好的。还要别的吗?
Hǎo de. Hái yào biéde ma?
Sure. Anything else?
A 不要了，谢谢。多少钱?
Bú yào le, xièxie. Duōshao qián?
No thanks. How much is it?

Patterns used: modal 要 (14), number + measure word (17), 吗 question (6), 了 for a change of mind (11).

C. Asking for directions

A 请问，附近有银行吗?
Qǐngwèn, fùjìn yǒu yínháng ma?
Excuse me, is there a bank nearby?
B 有，就在商店旁边。
Yǒu, jiù zài shāngdiàn pángbiān.
Yes, right next to the shop.
A 谢谢! 请说慢一点儿。
Xièxie! Qǐng shuō màn yìdiǎnr.
Thank you! Please speak a bit more slowly.

Patterns used: 有 for existence (4), 在 for location (5), 请 for politeness (20).

A twenty-minute daily routine

  1. 5 min — review. Clear today's spaced-repetition queue before anything new.
  2. 5 min — new material. Five to ten new characters or words, never more.
  3. 5 min — sentences. Write two sentences per new word using a pattern above.
  4. 5 min — sound. Read the sentences aloud and record yourself once a week.

Success check: after a week you can produce yesterday's words in a sentence without looking. If not, cut the new-material step in half.

Caveat · study timingsThe twenty-minute split is a practical starting point, not a research finding. Spaced repetition and distributed practice are well supported in general learning research, but no study establishes an optimal daily schedule specifically for Chinese characters.

07

Alternatives

Other routes to the same 1000

This guide follows an HSK-ordered route because it has an official syllabus and abundant graded material. It is not the only sensible order. Pick the route that matches why you are learning.

Frequency-first

Learn characters in raw corpus-frequency order, for example from Jun Da's Modern Chinese Character Frequency List.

Good for: reading native text as early as possible.
Trade-off: the first hundred include function words that are hard to use in isolation, and the order ignores what beginner textbooks teach.

HSK-ordered (this guide)

Follow the HSK 3.0 bands: 300 characters at Level 1, 600 by Level 2, 900 by Level 3, 1200 by Level 4.

Good for: exam candidates and anyone who wants graded readers and textbooks that match.
Trade-off: tied to one exam's editorial choices; HSK 2.0 material is still widely sold.

Textbook-led

Work through a single course such as an integrated Chinese-language series, learning whatever characters the chapters introduce.

Good for: learners who want grammar, audio, and characters sequenced together.
Trade-off: slower character coverage; you depend on one author's ordering.

Sentence mining

Harvest sentences from material you actually read or watch, and add only the characters those sentences need.

Good for: intermediate learners with a clear content interest.
Trade-off: a beginner has no material to mine yet; it works better after roughly 300 characters.

Component-first

Learn the most productive components and radicals before whole characters, then treat each character as a small assembly.

Good for: visual learners and anyone who feels characters are arbitrary.
Trade-off: delays useful vocabulary; best used alongside another route, not instead of it.

Recognition-only

Skip handwriting and train reading and typing (pinyin input) only.

Good for: travel, reading, and messaging; roughly doubles apparent progress speed.
Trade-off: weaker recall of similar-looking characters, and no help in a handwriting exam.

Tools worth having

  • A pop-up dictionary. Pleco is the reference mobile dictionary; the open CC-CEDICT database sits behind many free tools.
  • Stroke-order animation. Hanzi Writer is an open-source library that animates and quizzes stroke order.
  • Grammar reference. The Chinese Grammar Wiki indexes patterns by level with example sentences.
  • Spaced repetition. Any scheduler that shows you a card just before you would forget it. The principle is well evidenced; the specific app matters far less.

Caveat · tool listingsTool listings are descriptive, not endorsements, and the market changes quickly. Verify pricing, platform support, and data licensing yourself before committing to one.

08

Troubleshooting

When it stops working

Most beginner problems are a rung of the ladder that was skipped. Find the symptom, then go back one rung.

  • "I recognise the character but cannot recall the sound."

    Likely cause You learned the shape and the English meaning but never the pinyin as part of the same item.

    Fix Re-make the card so that the answer side always contains character, pinyin with tone, and meaning together, and say the word aloud when you answer.

  • "Similar characters keep blurring together."

    Likely cause You are memorising whole shapes instead of components.

    Fix Split each confusable pair into components and name the difference: 你 nǐ has the person radical, 他 tā also has it but with a different phonetic side; 买 mǎi and 卖 mài differ by one stroke group on top.

  • "My tones vanish in real sentences."

    Likely cause Tones were drilled in isolation, not in pairs.

    Fix Practise two-syllable words rather than single characters, and learn the sandhi rules explicitly: third + third becomes second (你好 → ní hǎo), and 不 becomes bú before a fourth tone.

  • "I know 500 characters but cannot read a menu."

    Likely cause Character coverage is not comprehension. Knowing 1000 characters covers around 89% of running text, but the remaining 11% is spread through almost every sentence.

    Fix Shift study from characters to two-character words, and read graded material at your level rather than authentic text.

  • "My sentences are word-for-word English."

    Likely cause Vocabulary grew faster than patterns.

    Fix Stop adding words for a week. Take ten patterns from section 05 and write five sentences each using words you already own.

  • "The reviews have become unmanageable."

    Likely cause Too many new items per day; a queue grows roughly ten times the daily intake.

    Fix Cap new items at five to ten a day, clear the backlog before adding anything, and drop handwriting to recognition-only if you are short of time.

  • "Two sources give different traditional characters."

    Likely cause You are mixing scripts and regions. Mainland China and Singapore use simplified; Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau use traditional, and standard word choices differ too.

    Fix Choose one target region first. Convert later — the simplified-to-traditional mapping is not always one to one.

  • "My textbook's level does not match the app's."

    Likely cause HSK 2.0 (six levels) and HSK 3.0 (nine levels) are both in circulation.

    Fix Check which standard a resource follows before trusting its level labels, and treat any single "HSK 1 character list" as approximate.

09

Next steps

After the first thousand

Push to 2000 characters

The second thousand takes you from roughly 89% to roughly 97% coverage of running text. Progress feels slower per character but reading feels dramatically easier.

Switch from characters to words

Most modern Chinese words are two characters. Once you have 1000 characters, add words rather than characters: you already know the parts.

Read graded material daily

Graded readers at HSK 3 to 4 turn recognition into fluency. Aim for text where you understand about 95% without a dictionary.

Add listening at natural speed

Reading progress hides listening gaps. Short podcasts for learners, replayed several times, close the gap faster than more flashcards.

Take a level test

If you want an external checkpoint, HSK 3 is a realistic first exam at 900 characters. Check the current official syllabus before booking.

Decide about handwriting

If you skipped handwriting, this is the point to decide. Writing 200 to 300 core characters by hand sharpens recognition of everything else.

Plain-language glossary

汉字 hànzì
Chinese characters, the writing units themselves.
Pinyin pīnyīn
The official romanisation of Mandarin sounds.
Tone
One of four pitch contours, plus a neutral tone, that distinguish meaning.
Tone sandhi
A predictable tone change caused by a neighbouring tone.
Stroke
A single pen movement; the smallest unit of a character.
Component
A reusable sub-shape inside a character, such as 女 or 口.
Radical bùshǒu
The component under which a character is filed in a dictionary.
形声字 xíngshēngzì
A character combining a meaning hint with a sound hint.
Measure word
A classifier required between a number and a noun, such as 个.
HSK
The state-backed Chinese proficiency test and its syllabus.
Simplified / Traditional
Two standard character sets used in different regions.
Coverage
The share of running text tokens a character set accounts for.
